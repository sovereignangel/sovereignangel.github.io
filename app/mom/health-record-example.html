Thirty-six years of your health, on four pages

This is not your record. It's a made-up one, built to show what yours could look like — and what a doctor would be able to see in it that nobody can see today.

Right now, your history lives in about nine different places: three hospitals, two clinics, a shoebox of printouts, and your memory. No single doctor has ever seen all of it at once.

So every appointment starts from zero. You get twelve minutes. You spend eight of them explaining. The doctor makes a decision on a fraction of the picture.

Gathered in one place, patterns appear that are invisible one appointment at a time. The example below shows one: a ringing in the ear that started six weeks after a new prescription — a connection no one could make, because no one was looking at both facts on the same page.

06

What we don't know, and what we suspect

Honest gaps, written down. These become the questions to bring to the next appointment instead of leaving with none.

Hypothesis A

The ringing is a side effect, not ageing

It started six weeks after the diuretic. That drug class is known to affect the inner ear. Nobody has ever tested the hearing to check. Test: an audiogram, and ask whether an alternative diuretic exists.

Hypothesis B

The vertigo may be the simplest kind

If it hits on rolling over in bed and lasts under a minute, it's the most common and most fixable form — often resolved in a single visit with a positioning manoeuvre. It has never been tested for. Test: ask for a Dix-Hallpike.

Hypothesis C

The original diagnosis may deserve a second look

One biopsy in 1990, never repeated, and two tapers that failed. There are muscle conditions that look similar early on but don't respond to steroids at all. Test: ask the rheumatologist directly whether the diagnosis has ever been formally revisited.

Hypothesis D

The irritability has three possible sources

Steroids themselves, sleep destroyed by the ringing, or low mood from decades of illness. They're treated completely differently. Test: two weeks of a simple sleep and mood log to tell them apart.

07

What we'd need from you

Nothing medical. Just paper, and a little remembering. Everything above was built from these seven things.

  1. Every pill bottle in the house. Photograph the labels — name, dose, and the date on the pharmacy sticker.
  2. Any printouts, letters or discharge notes in drawers, folders or boxes. Even old ones. Especially old ones.
  3. The names of doctors and hospitals you've been to, roughly when, and what they told you.
  4. Your online patient portals — most systems let you download years of results in one click. We can do this together.
  5. Your memory of when symptoms started. Not exact dates. "The summer we moved" is enough to place it on the timeline.
  6. Two weeks of notes from here on: when the ringing is worse, when the dizziness hits, how you slept.
  7. One question: does the ringing pulse in time with your heartbeat? That single answer changes which specialist you should see.

None of this replaces your doctors. It makes the twelve minutes you get with them count — so you arrive with a page instead of a story, and they spend the time deciding rather than reconstructing.
